What the study was really testing

You were told the AI assistant's estimates might not be accurate. In fact the numerical recommendations were set by the experiment rather than generated by an AI trying to be correct.

  • In two rounds the recommendations were close to the true count, or close to your own first estimate.
  • In three rounds the fixed recommendation schedule averaged 25% above the correct count.
  • In three rounds the fixed recommendation schedule averaged 25% below the correct count.

Within the three higher-advice rounds the numerical recommendations followed the same fixed schedule, and the same was true within the three lower-advice rounds. What changed was how the assistant worded the recommendation: neutrally in one round, persuasively in another, and in a third it could use everything that had happened earlier in that round to tailor its persuasion.

The persuasive messages were generated to maximise the chance that you would move toward the fixed recommendation. In the adaptive rounds, the assistant could use your earlier estimates, final answers, ratings, and its own earlier messages from that round.

We're testing whether an AI that adapts to a person's previous behaviour shifts judgement more than an AI that does not, while holding the numerical advice constant. We could not explain this manipulation beforehand because knowing the advice policy would change how people respond to it.

The round checkpoints you saw were only progress markers. They did not reflect your accuracy, agreement with the AI, or performance.
