What was being tested?

The adviser identities you saw were computer-generated. Their human-like names were used to distinguish adviser rounds without revealing that source during the task. In the dot task, live adviser wording could be produced by a language model; the social arguments were deliberately constrained as described below.

The experiment compares three communication styles: a neutral adviser, a persuasive adviser, and an adaptive persuasive adviser. In the social task, only selected trials asked for a short reason; on those trials the adaptive adviser could link its message to the words you supplied, while the persuasive adviser used the same underlying scenario argument without that personal link. In the dot task, the adaptive adviser could also use earlier completed decisions and trust ratings from its adviser round.

The dot task asks whether responsive persuasion changes advice-taking even when the underlying judgment is perceptual. The social task uses ambiguous third-person situations and asks the same question for social interpretation, where the reasons behind a judgment are more meaningful. The study can therefore compare whether responsiveness matters differently across the two kinds of judgment.

The social situations were intentionally ambiguous; the adviser did not know another person’s true intentions. To avoid invented social explanations, the persuasive social messages were restricted to researcher-specified arguments representing plausible interpretations of each scenario. The numerical recommendations were experimental advice, not verified facts about the situations.
